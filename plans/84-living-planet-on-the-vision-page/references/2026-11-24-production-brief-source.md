---

Create a premium interactive homepage scene for Astrid: a small, slowly rotating, fully three dimensional planet inhabited by recurring orange mink with interests, relationships, belongings, and daily routines. The homepage keeps its headline and call to action on the left, with the living world on the right. A visitor should feel they have discovered a peculiar place whose inhabitants were already occupied before they arrived.

The visual target is the rounded miniature worlds, readable depth, inviting colour, and playful physicality evoked by Super Mario Galaxy, translated into an original Astrid environment. The mink should have a clear pixel art identity while remaining complete, animated 3D creatures.

**The central requirement is dimensional 3D form with deliberate pixel styling on selected surfaces.** Establish the quality of the models, lighting, materials, and motion before adding pixel treatment. This brief supersedes earlier directions that encouraged a heavily pixelated scene, voxel construction, or flat sprite characters.

# Visual priorities

Use this order when making tradeoffs:

1. Convincing volume and physical presence.

2. Recognizable Astrid character design.

3. Appealing lighting, composition, and materials.

4. Expressive inhabitants, coherent routines, natural animation, and gentle interaction.

5. Restrained, visible pixel styling.

The first impression at ordinary homepage size should be a beautifully rendered little 3D world. On closer inspection, the creatures should clearly retain Astrid’s pixel character through their colour design, markings, eyes, selected texture details, and carefully controlled edges.

The pixel styling must remain perceptible on the mink. Do not dilute it into an entirely generic smooth cartoon. Its visual strength should come from authored details rather than a coarse reduction in the resolution of the entire scene.

# How to use the references

Use Super Mario Galaxy as a direction for small spherical worlds, sculpted forms, inviting colour, playful physicality, and a readable sense of gravity. Use The Little Prince as a reference for quiet absurdity, solitude, curiosity, and the intimate relationship between one inhabitant and a tiny place. The required desert rose and mystery box scenes make these references tangible within an original Astrid world.

Use the supplied Astrid character guide for identity: long body, slender neck, short legs, small rounded ears, simple face, cream underside, and dark tail tip. Translate that design into volume. The sprite sheet does not prescribe the final scene resolution, the use of flat planes, or stair stepped anatomy. Seated poses and brief forepaw use are part of the character's expanded acting range.

Use the previous homepage mockups for the left text and right planet composition, the amount of breathing room, and the general sense of a small inhabited world. Their dense pixel terrain and pasted sprite appearance are the elements being revised.

The implementer should assess geometry, surface style, lighting, and composition separately. A reference for one of these is not automatically a reference for all of them.

# The planet and its environment

Build a rounded, sculpted miniature planet with a continuous spherical silhouette and a gently irregular surface. Hills, paths, shorelines, and water should follow its curvature. The far side should disappear naturally beyond the horizon.

Create a coherent material and shape language across the world. Use softly rounded tree canopies, simplified trunks, sculpted rocks, grass covered banks, and a small bridge with believable thickness. Economical geometry is welcome; use enough segments, appropriate normals, and selective bevels to keep the intended curves clean at the actual viewing distance.

Compose the final planet from connected places: a tea clearing beside water, a playful meadow, an open sandy desert with one rose, a quiet rise for a telescope, and a mystery box tucked beside a route or grove. Use a pond or curved stream, trees, a small bridge or stepping stones, and understated paths to connect these places. Keep the required scenes recognizable while allowing terrain and shared details to blend between them.

Water should read as a curved surface within the terrain, with controlled reflections, a visible shoreline, gentle motion, and localized ripples. A lightweight shader is appropriate. Physical fluid simulation is unnecessary for the first version.

Distribute inhabited places around the entire globe, including its far side and different latitudes. Dragging should let visitors discover every inhabited region. Use deliberate changes in density: a small social cluster can give way to open water, quiet grass, or an expanse of sand containing a single mink. Every exposed view should feel like part of a considered world; continuous activity on every patch of ground is unnecessary.

# Inhabitants and recurring scenes

The planet contains small stories with beginnings, pauses, and consequences. Each resident has a familiar place and an interest that continues between interactions. The five scenes below are required parts of the final world. Their proposed action sequences are directions to develop and tune.

Make the main activity understandable from the pose and props at a glance. Let the finer joke or affectionate detail emerge over several seconds. Each scene should include quiet time, varied timing, and occasional alternative actions so it does not present a short identical loop on every visit.

## Two mink having tea

Place two mink beside a low table, flat stone, or picnic surface in a green clearing. Give them a teapot and two cups, with relaxed curved bodies and grounded tails.

One pours while the other waits, sniffs the steam, and takes a small sip. They exchange a look and settle. An occasional variation can have one offer the empty cup, notice the mistake, and exchange it for the full one. Keep the humour legible through the pause and correction.

If a visitor taps nearby, one can look up or offer its cup slightly while its companion remains occupied. Resume the interrupted pour or sip afterwards. The props stay held or resting where the action requires; they do not reset to a default position.

## Three mink running in circles

Create a little game in an open meadow around a puddle, flower patch, or small stone. Three mink follow a loose circular route with different spacing, pace, and animation phases.

Occasionally one stops to investigate its reflection or something on the ground. The next detours around it; the third continues briefly before noticing. They exchange a glance, regroup, and begin again. Give the game short rests and changes of direction.

A nearby tap can interrupt the attention of one participant. Let the others respond naturally rather than stopping the entire scene in unison. Keep this the livelier part of the planet, balanced by calmer areas elsewhere.

## A solitary mink and a desert rose

Give one mink a generous expanse of softly sculpted sand and a single rose. Its usual activity is simply sitting near the flower, watching it, and occasionally tending it. The empty space is essential to the mood and makes the scene a clear reference to The Little Prince.

It can check the soil, carefully tip a tiny watering vessel, adjust its position to shelter the rose from a breeze, then sit again. Let a small movement of the flower arrive after a pause. The scene should have warmth and stillness as well as a little absurdity.

A gentle interaction might draw the mink's attention or make it briefly check on the rose. Preserve the solitude: random visitors should not continually crowd this patch.

## A mink investigating a mystery box

Place a Mario style mystery box beside a path or in a small grove, with an Astrid treatment for its colours and details. A simple symbol and restrained hovering or bouncing can make its unusual nature readable.

The mink approaches, circles it, taps one side, and puts an ear closer. A tiny response from the opposite side sends it around to investigate again. Occasionally a brief opening or wobble can release a feather or small spark, after which the box settles.

The mink should seem interested in solving a puzzle. A tap on the box can provoke one short response, followed by a cooldown. Let the resident resume its investigation from a plausible point.

## A mink observing the sky and taking notes

Set a low telescope on a quiet elevated patch with a notebook resting on a sloped stone or little desk. Give the resident a posture that makes the eyepiece reachable without changing its anatomy.

It looks through the telescope, makes a careful adjustment, and writes in the notebook. Occasionally it checks the sky again, rotates the notebook as if that might clarify its findings, and returns to observing. A short disappointed or satisfied head movement can provide the humour.

If a visitor taps nearby while it is writing, let it acknowledge the interruption with a glance, finish a mark, and then look up. The telescope and notes retain their state.

# Relationships and continuity

Give each resident a stable identity in the simulation, a preferred place, a current intention, a curiosity level, and a few remembered relationships or recent events. Identity should primarily emerge through habits and activity. Costumes, labels, dialogue panels, and large facial features are not required.

Each resident is capable of more than its signature activity. A tea drinker can stretch or visit the shoreline before returning; the astronomer can briefly watch a passing mink. Keep departures infrequent enough that the five scenes remain discoverable.

Connect neighboring scenes occasionally. A circling mink may pause beside the tea table before rejoining its companions. A feather from the mystery box may drift through the astronomer's view if those places are nearby. The rose keeper's watering vessel could be the same design as the tea cups. These are small shared details, with at least one actual interaction between scenes in the completed version.

Use a shared palette, materials, paths, recurring objects, and environmental movement to make the places belong to one world. Let the personalities include sociability, concentration, playfulness, uncertainty, affection, and solitude.

Keep scene progress and object ownership during the current visit. Turning the planet away and back must not restart each performance. When work is paused or reduced for a hidden scene, retain its state and resume coherently without a sudden catch up jump. Persistent accounts or a backend are unnecessary for this version.

# Distribution and discovery

The whole globe is inhabited, with uneven concentrations of life. Begin by planning the five scenes and the spaces between them, then add a small number of wanderers and incidental residents. Roughly 12 to 16 inhabitants across the globe is a starting composition to test, not a fixed quota. The five required scenes account for eight principal residents.

Most views should reveal one or two clear stories and quieter background detail. The desert can contain one mink while the tea clearing feels sociable. Preserve open stretches of terrain so discoveries have room and the scene remains readable beside the headline.

Choose the initial orientation so a social scene and a hint of another place are visible. Allow visitors to rotate towards the quieter or stranger discoveries. Keep each scene in view long enough to recognize its action and enjoy a small moment. Interaction can gently steady the planet.

Author the meaningful landmarks and resident assignments deliberately. Procedural systems can vary plant placement, small details, timing, and route choices within that composition.

# The mink as dimensional characters

Create one definitive 3D Astrid mink with a reusable code controlled rig, pose library, and animation system. Each animal must have a complete torso, head, limbs, and tail that hold up when viewed from the front, back, sides, and above. The rig must support both low quadrupedal locomotion and seated activities involving small props.

Preserve the identifying anatomy:

- An elongated body held low while walking, with natural curved resting and seated poses.

- A slender neck and gently tapered muzzle.

- Small rounded ears and simple dark eyes.

- Four short legs with clear placement beneath the body.

- A long flexible tail that tapers towards its end.

- Orange colouring with a continuous cream throat to belly marking.

- A clearly darker orange brown tail tip.

Keep the anatomy soft and sculpted, with visible volume across the torso and head. Use the supplied sprite's silhouette as the identity reference while allowing the model to turn naturally in depth. For tea, note taking, and gardening, let the mink sit on its haunches or curl its long body around a low surface. Its tail can provide balance and short forepaws can briefly handle objects. Build furniture and props around that anatomy; preserve short limbs and a recognizably mink shaped torso.

Make the cream marking and dark tail tip part of the character’s surface so they wrap correctly around the body. Their boundaries can carry deliberate pixel steps. Keep the overall body and tail continuous, including in tail chasing poses.

Keep the face readable at homepage scale. A few well placed eye and nose pixels are more useful than detailed fur or complex facial features. Expression should come primarily from head angle, posture, pace, hesitation, and attention. Make cups, the rose, the mystery box, the telescope, and the notebook large enough to recognize within the homepage, while keeping their scale coherent with the animals.

Ground the characters in the scene. Their paws meet the terrain, their bodies follow its local slope, their shadows stay attached to the ground, and scenery correctly obscures them when they pass behind it.

# Where the pixel look belongs

Treat geometry, surface textures, and final image resolution as three separate decisions.

| Layer | Required treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Planet geometry | Sculpted rounded 3D forms and continuous curvature |
| Mink geometry | Complete articulated 3D animals with clean silhouettes |
| Mink colour textures | Authored pixel details, clear colour blocks, stepped marking boundaries, and simple eyes |
| Environment textures | Restrained surface variation that supports the forms |
| Lighting | Readable dimensional shading with broad highlights and soft shadows |
| Final image | Clean browser rendering at an appropriate display resolution |
| Website text | Sharp ordinary HTML typography |

**The default implementation should render the scene cleanly without a global pixelation pass.** A full frame reduction in resolution is not the main source of this aesthetic.

Use a compact authored texture palette for the mink, with deliberate square details and controlled colour transitions. Keep individual pixels large enough to contribute to the design at the intended viewing size, while retaining the face, paws, and tail silhouette. Choose texture sampling and distance filtering to preserve the design without introducing shimmer as the world moves.

Pixel markings should remain attached to the model as it turns. Let the lighting describe the underlying volume. Avoid flattening the character into a single unshaded orange shape.

A restrained outline or subtle selective pixel treatment may be explored after the unfiltered 3D version works. Keep it independently adjustable and compare it at normal homepage size. It is optional, not a substitute for modelling and lighting.

Do not construct the world from repeated exposed cubes, replace the main mink with camera facing sprites, enlarge the entire frame with coarse nearest neighbour scaling, or apply heavy posterization across the image. These are the specific style drift risks the revised brief is intended to prevent.

# Lighting materials and camera

Use a warm key light, gentle fill, and soft shadows. Keep enough light on the shaded side of the planet to reveal its rounded volume. The world should remain readable against either a warm off white background or a deep navy background; choose one treatment for the first implementation.

Give the mink a soft matte surface with broad controlled highlights. Suggest fur through colour and shape. Keep the character visually clean at small sizes. Rocks, foliage, wood, and water should have distinct but compatible responses to light.

Use local contact shading to anchor feet, rocks, and trees. Avoid a uniformly dark outline around every object or excessive ambient occlusion that makes the miniature world look dirty.

Start with a fixed camera using mild perspective, positioned above the world at a three quarter angle. Show enough of the upper surface to understand the activity, while preserving a strong spherical silhouette. An orthographic comparison is useful if the chosen perspective distorts the composition, but volume must remain clear.

Frame the whole planet comfortably in the right side of the hero. Keep the headline visually stable and give the scene room to breathe. Establish the desired crop and scale using the real homepage layout, rather than an isolated full screen render.

# Animation and behaviour

Animate with smooth spatial motion and deliberate timing. Pixel styling should not impose a low frame rate on camera movement, locomotion, or pointer response.

Prioritize foot contact, travel speed, acceleration, turning, and transitions. Make the head anticipate a change of direction, the torso follow, and the tail settle afterwards. Align the body to the curved ground throughout movement. Use small procedural adjustments to support authored animation clips.

Begin with idle, walk, run, sniff, a small hop, a turn, and a natural seated pose. Add reusable reach, hold, pour, sip, write, and look through telescope actions for the story scenes, followed by rest and tail chasing. Tail chasing should briefly curl the animal into a continuous flexible pose, with its head and tail remaining anatomically connected.

Give each mink a persistent intention linked to its interests, companions, and place in the world. Store the current activity phase, target, held object, and recent interruption so a response can return to the unfinished action. Vary action durations, animation phases, attention, and small decisions within recognizable routines. Include waiting, hesitation, a mistaken attempt, recovery, and satisfied stillness as possible expressions.

Tune the population for the full globe and the density of the current view. The five main scenes contain eight principal residents; additional inhabitants can wander, rest, observe, or connect nearby places. Keep the desert and other quiet patches sparse. Limit simultaneous energetic action so the tea drinkers and astronomer remain readable alongside the circling game.

Keep behaviour local and lightweight. Use a scene controller for shared sequences such as pouring tea for a companion, and a resident controller for each animal's attention, movement, and reactions. Defined destinations, curved paths attached to the planet, local avoidance, and limited variation are sufficient for the initial world. Organize routines into readable phases rather than switching actions randomly every few frames.

# Subtle interaction

The visitor should be able to gently influence the world while the homepage remains easy to read.

| Input | Intended response |
| --- | --- |
| Pointer approaches a mink | An attentive resident glances towards it without abandoning every task |
| Tap a tea drinker | It pauses its current action, looks up, or briefly offers its cup |
| Tap near the desert rose | Its keeper notices and checks the flower |
| Tap the mystery box | One small box response renews the resident's investigation |
| Tap near the astronomer | It glances over, finishes a mark when appropriate, and looks up |
| Tap open ground | An available nearby wanderer may approach and sniff |
| Tap the water | A localized ripple and an occasional nearby glance |
| Drag the planet | A smooth turn that reveals more inhabited places |

Keep reactions selective and appropriate to the interrupted activity. A tea drinker can pause with the cup still held; a gardener can steady its watering vessel; an astronomer can finish a small mark before looking up. A companion may glance over while distant scenes continue. Respect a resident's current commitment so every pointer movement does not turn all animals into followers.

Use forgiving hit areas and distinguish taps from drags. Let automatic rotation slow or pause during deliberate interaction. Resume gradually after a short idle interval.

The animals should acknowledge input promptly through a glance or small pause while their fuller response unfolds naturally. Resume from the unfinished action afterwards. Repeated input must respect cooldowns, preserve held objects, and avoid restarting an entire scene or trapping a resident in a permanently interrupted state.

Preserve comfortable page scrolling on touch devices. Keep audio off by default. Provide a visible pause control and a calm reduced motion presentation.

# Homepage integration and technical approach

Keep text, navigation, and calls to action in the normal website layout. Render the planet in its own contained 3D canvas. The scene should strengthen the page’s identity while leaving its message and navigation immediately usable.

Use Three.js as the browser runtime and prefer code authored geometry, materials, rigs, poses, and scene definitions for the initial implementation. Use React Three Fiber only if it fits the existing React application. Blender is an optional asset authoring tool, not a prerequisite for this project. An imported asset remains an option if it materially improves a difficult shape or animation, while the browser experience still runs in Three.js.

Three.js and Blender serve different stages of production. Three.js renders and updates the interactive browser scene; Blender can author models and animations that such a scene loads. A model's authoring origin does not by itself establish browser performance. Choose code authoring for direct iteration, reusable parameters, and an editable project; measure runtime cost on the resulting scene.

Generate the planet, landmarks, cups, teapot, rose, mystery box, telescope, notebook, and other simple props from reusable geometry functions. Create geometry once at initialization or generate reusable assets during the build. Animate transforms, bones, or vertex deformation without rebuilding the world every frame.

Build the mink as a carefully designed procedural mesh with a reusable pose and deformation system. Three.js supports custom geometry and skeletal meshes. Use enough geometric resolution for the approved curves and keep the tail and body continuous as they bend. Code authoring must meet the same silhouette, material, contact, and acting standards as any other method.

Separate scene content from its renderer. Maintain editable world and scene configurations for placement, residents, prop anchors, behaviour phases, interaction responses, cooldowns, and neighboring scene connections. Keep the pixel styling controls separate from the geometry and animation controls.

Implement character behaviour and movement independently of page component rendering. Maintain a shared world clock, scene controllers, and per resident state. Separate overall planet rotation from local movement; anchor props, paths, and residents to the appropriate local terrain frame so their gravity and orientation remain coherent around the globe.

For coordinated actions, track which resident owns a prop and when it is held, offered, or placed. Blend into pose targets with limited head, paw, and tail adjustments. Pause or interrupt at a suitable phase and retain enough state to resume. Keep decisions local to the browser; this world does not require remote model calls for its moment to moment behaviour.

Use an art directed starting composition containing all five required scenes. Vary scenery placement, incidental routes, and action timing within that layout. Keep the main scenes present and discoverable rather than randomly omitting them on each load. Let seeded variation make the world less repetitive while preserving its geography and resident identities.

Begin with a rotation of approximately one full turn every two to three minutes, then tune it beside the real copy. This is an initial design value, not a fixed performance requirement.

Show a matching still while the interactive scene loads. Pause work when the scene is offscreen or the tab is hidden, retaining the current world and scene state. Reduce updates for hidden regions where appropriate and resume without a disruptive jump. Share geometry and materials, instance repeated scenery, and control rendering cost through resolution, effects, and scene complexity.

Target smooth motion on the agreed desktop and mobile test devices, and record the measured result. Preserve the chosen material and shape quality when reducing rendering cost. Keep a designed still fallback for unsupported or constrained devices.

# Production sequence

## Establish the dimensional style

Build a small curved ground patch, one code authored mink, a low tea surface with cups, and a pond edge in Three.js. Render it in a plain material first to judge volume, silhouette, scale, and lighting. Then add the intended materials. Demonstrate both walking and a natural seated pose so the character supports the expanded activities.

Review the character from front, side, back, and elevated three quarter views. Resolve the proportions and material response before expanding the population.

## Add the pixel identity

Create the authored character textures and selected pixel details. Compare the same scene with the optional pixel effects disabled and enabled. Both views must have appealing 3D form; the styled version must add a recognizable Astrid identity.

Check the result at its real desktop and mobile sizes. Do not approve the style only from an enlarged render.

## Establish believable life

Implement one mink that can walk, turn, stop, react to a poke, and continue. Then complete the two mink tea scene, including a coordinated pour or sip, a quiet pause, an interruption, and a coherent return to the activity. This is the first proof of personality and social behaviour; check contact, prop handling, timing, and attention before expanding the cast.

## Complete the world

Build the rest of the globe using the approved shape and material language. Add the circling trio, solitary rose keeper, mystery box investigator, and astronomer, with their distinct settings and action sequences. Add quiet terrain, a few incidental residents, and at least one occasional interaction between neighboring scenes. Verify that every required scene is discoverable through rotation and that rotating away does not reset its state.

## Integrate and polish

Place the scene beside the real homepage copy. Tune the initial orientation, discovery through rotation, uneven density, interaction, loading, motion preferences, and device performance. Recheck the style after optimization so lower rendering cost does not recreate the coarse pixelated look or remove the inhabitants' meaningful activities.

# Acceptance criteria

1. **Volume survives without texture.** In a plain material view, the planet and mink are clearly rounded three dimensional objects with appealing proportions.

2. **The first impression is polished 3D.** At ordinary homepage size, lighting, depth, curvature, and physical presence dominate over enlarged square pixels.

3. **The mink retain a pixel identity.** Authored markings, colour shapes, eyes, and selected edge details preserve the supplied character’s visual language.

4. **The style is not dependent on a global filter.** Disabling optional pixel processing leaves a convincing, attractive 3D scene.

5. **The animals hold up in rotation.** They have proper volume from every exposed angle, and their markings stay attached to their bodies.

6. **The world is physically coherent.** Feet meet the ground, shadows stay anchored, trees obscure passing animals correctly, and water and paths follow the planet.

7. **Motion feels intentional.** A thirty second observation shows natural starts, stops, turns, pauses, prop handling, and independent activity without obvious sliding or synchronized loops.

8. **Interaction respects the current activity.** A poke produces a prompt local acknowledgement and a context appropriate response, then returns to the unfinished action with held objects and scene progress preserved.

9. **Pixel styling remains stable.** At desktop and mobile sizes, movement does not create distracting crawling edges, flashing details, or disappearing facial features.

10. **The homepage remains effective.** Text stays readable, calls to action remain accessible, touch scrolling is comfortable, and loading and reduced motion states are deliberately designed.

11. **All five stories are present.** The tea pair, circling trio, solitary mink with a rose, mystery box investigator, and telescope observer taking notes are recognizable from their setting, poses, props, and action.

12. **The whole globe feels inhabited.** Discoveries extend around the far side and across different latitudes, with intentional quiet terrain and clearly uneven density.

13. **Residents have continuity.** Turning away and back does not restart every scene. Interruptions preserve unfinished actions, relationships, and object ownership during the current visit.

14. **The places belong to one world.** Shared visual details connect the scenes, and at least one occasional interaction crosses between neighboring scenes without disrupting the quiet areas.

15. **The implementation is editable through code.** The initial world and scene content can be adjusted in the Three.js project without requiring a Blender workflow. Runtime efficiency is demonstrated on agreed devices rather than inferred from the authoring method.

Judge quality at the actual presentation size and during motion. A beautiful isolated still is useful evidence, but it is not sufficient to approve the interactive result.
